# Issue 5: Anti-AI Writing

*How to spot and kill the AI tells.*

---

You can smell AI writing from a mile away.

Something about it feels off. Smooth in a way that reads as slippery. Confident without conviction. Perfectly structured but saying nothing.

Most people can't articulate what's wrong. They just know it when they see it.

I spent three months documenting exactly what's wrong. Every pattern. Every tell. Every sentence structure that screams "a robot wrote this."

---

The #1 AI tell is the correlative construction.

"X isn't just Y - it's Z."

"Teachers aren't just instructors - they're mentors."

"This tool isn't just useful - it's essential."

You see this everywhere. Every AI-generated blog post. Every ChatGPT email. It's the default structure the models reach for. And once you notice it, you can't unsee it.

The fix is simple: say what you mean directly. "Teachers mentor students." Done.

---

The #2 AI tell is hedge words.

"This might help you improve your workflow."

"It could be argued that..."

"Perhaps we should consider..."

AI loves hedges because they make uncertain claims safer. But they make writing weak.

The fix: commit. "This will help you." "Here's the argument." "We should do this."

If you're not certain enough to remove the hedge, you shouldn't be writing the sentence.

---

The #3 AI tell is empty flourishes.

"The best part?"

"Here's the thing..."

"Let that sink in."

"Now more than ever..."

These phrases do zero work. They're verbal throat-clearing. Space-fillers that give the illusion of meaning.

The fix: delete them. If your sentence needs "The best part?" to create anticipation, the sentence isn't interesting enough.

---

The #4 AI tell is passive voice.

"It was determined that changes were needed."

"The project has been completed."

"It has been shown that..."

Who determined? Who completed? Who showed? AI defaults to passive because it's safer. It hides the actor. It removes commitment.

The fix: name names. "We decided to change." "I finished the project." "Studies show."

---

The #5 AI tell is corporate jargon.

"Leveraging synergies across stakeholder ecosystems."

This one is partly AI's fault and partly ours. The models learned from corporate writing. When you ask for "professional" tone, you get this garbage.

The fix: write like you talk. "Getting different people talking to each other."

---

Here's the test I use before publishing:

For every sentence, ask: "Would this appear in a ChatGPT output?"

If yes, rewrite it.

That's it. The bar is that simple. If a sentence sounds like something AI would produce, it's a tell.

---

This sounds tedious. It's not.

Once you internalize the patterns, you catch them automatically. You start writing differently. The tells don't appear because you've trained yourself to avoid them.

It takes maybe two weeks. Then you're producing cleaner drafts without thinking about it.

---

The skill this week is an AI-tell detector.

Feed it any draft. It highlights the tells. Correlative constructions, hedge words, passive voice, corporate jargon, empty flourishes.

Red flags everywhere.

Then you fix them. Or you use the skill's rewrite suggestions. Either way, the output is cleaner.

---

A note on why this matters:

Reader trust is collapsing. Everyone's suspicious of AI content. They're developing antibodies.

If your writing triggers those antibodies, they stop reading. Doesn't matter if the ideas are good. They never get there.

Clean writing - writing that passes the "would AI produce this?" test - earns a longer leash. Readers give you the benefit of the doubt. They keep reading.

---

The irony is that I use AI constantly.

Voice memos to transcripts. Rough drafts to polished prose. Outlines to full posts. Claude is in every step.

But the output doesn't read like AI. Because I've trained myself to catch the tells. Because I have skills that enforce my voice. Because I review every sentence.

AI as collaborator, not author. That's the goal.
