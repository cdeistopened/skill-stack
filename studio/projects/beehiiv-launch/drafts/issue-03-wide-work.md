# Issue 3: Wide Work

*Cal Newport was right. Until AI.*

---

Deep Work was the bible for a decade.

One task. Total focus. No distractions. The monk in his cell, writing code or prose for hours without interruption.

I believed it. I built my whole system around it. Morning blocks. Phone in another room. Apps blocked. The works.

Then Claude happened.

---

Here's what I noticed: my best AI sessions aren't deep. They're wide.

I'm not focused on one thing. I'm conducting multiple threads. I'll have Claude working on a blog post while I review a transcript while I sketch out a newsletter while I wait for a podcast edit to process.

It feels like cheating. Like I'm violating the sacred principles of deep work.

But the output is 3x what I produced in monk mode.

---

The insight: AI changes the nature of the bottleneck.

Before AI, the bottleneck was your focused attention. Deep work maximized output because every word, every line of code, every creative decision required your full brain.

After AI, the bottleneck shifts. The AI can handle the execution. Your job is orchestration.

And orchestration is inherently wide.

---

Think about a film director.

They're not doing deep work. They're managing dozens of simultaneous threads - cinematographer, actors, sound, lighting, continuity. Their job is integration, not isolation.

That's what AI work feels like. You're the conductor. Claude is the orchestra.

---

Here's what wide work looks like in practice:

**Thread 1:** Rough draft of a newsletter in progress. Claude is iterating on the intro. I check in every few minutes.

**Thread 2:** A transcript I need to turn into a blog post. I've fed it to a polisher skill. It's churning.

**Thread 3:** Five social media hooks I asked for. They arrived while I was checking Thread 1.

**Thread 4:** A research question I have. Claude is gathering context from my notes.

I'm not multitasking in the old sense. I'm not fragmenting my attention across unrelated tasks. All four threads serve the same goal. I'm just not waiting for each one to finish before starting the next.

---

The mental model that helps: zoom in, zoom out.

Zoom in to make a judgment call. "This intro is weak, try a different angle." "This transcript section is off-topic, cut it."

Zoom out to see the whole. "How do these pieces connect?" "What's the through-line?" "Am I missing something?"

The zooming is the skill. AI handles everything else.

---

This only works if you have the right infrastructure.

You need skills loaded so you're not explaining yourself every time. You need context files that persist your preferences. You need a system that remembers.

Without that, wide work becomes chaos. With it, wide work becomes leverage.

---

I still believe in deep work. But for different things.

Deep work is for thinking. For the ideas that require sustained attention. For the problems that can't be decomposed.

Wide work is for production. For the execution that follows thinking. For turning one idea into five outputs.

The best creators do both. They go deep to figure out what to say. They go wide to get it out the door.

---

The skill this week is an orchestration framework.

It's a checklist for managing multiple AI threads without losing track. What to check when. How to maintain context across threads. How to know when you're actually multitasking (bad) versus conducting (good).

---

The old model: "I can't get anything done because I'm interrupted all the time."

The new model: "I can't get anything done because I'm only running one thread at a time."

Both are true. The question is which bottleneck you're hitting.

If you're constantly interrupted by external distractions, deep work is the answer.

If you're constantly waiting for AI to finish one thing before starting the next, wide work is the answer.

---

Most people won't do this.

It feels uncomfortable. It violates everything they learned about focus. It seems like a recipe for scattered, low-quality work.

But the people who figure it out will produce at a different scale entirely.

Not because they're smarter. Because they're conducting while everyone else is still performing.
