# Issue 2: Learn to Flow

*Don't learn to code. Learn to flow.*

---

I've dictated over 900,000 words in the past two years.

Voice memos while driving. Notes while walking the dog. Brain dumps at 11pm when an idea won't let me sleep.

None of it looks pretty. Half-sentences. Tangents. The verbal equivalent of a first draft.

But here's the thing: that messy raw material is more valuable than anything I could type.

---

Most people approach AI writing backwards.

They sit at a keyboard, stare at a blank screen, and try to type something worth feeding to Claude.

The friction is enormous. You're trying to think and compose at the same time. Every sentence gets edited before it's finished. You second-guess yourself into paralysis.

Meanwhile, your best ideas happen in the shower. On a walk. In the car. Places where you can't type.

---

Voice changes everything.

When you talk, you don't edit. You just... go. Ideas flow in the order they want to flow. You follow tangents. You contradict yourself. You stumble onto insights you didn't know you had.

This is the raw material AI needs.

Not polished prose. Not carefully structured arguments. Just you, thinking out loud, captured.

---

Here's my workflow:

1. Walk and talk. Record a voice memo about whatever's on my mind.
2. Transcribe it. Whisper, Otter, or the built-in transcription on my phone.
3. Feed the transcript to Claude with a skill loaded.
4. Watch messy thinking become clear prose.

The transformation is almost magic. Twenty minutes of rambling becomes a tight 800-word essay. The ideas are mine. The structure is mine (once I figure out what I was actually trying to say). The polish comes from AI.

---

"But I don't think well out loud."

Yes you do. You just haven't practiced.

The first few voice memos will feel awkward. You'll trail off. You'll say "um" a lot. You'll hate the sound of your own voice.

Keep going.

After a week, something shifts. You start to hear yourself think. You notice patterns. You discover that your brain actually works pretty well when you stop interrupting it.

---

The technical term for this is "beginner's mind."

When you type, you're in editor mode. Judging every word as it appears. Trying to get it right the first time.

When you speak, you're in creator mode. Following the thread wherever it leads. Trusting that you'll figure it out.

Creator mode produces raw material. Editor mode refines it. Most people skip creator mode entirely. They try to edit something that doesn't exist yet.

---

English is the hottest programming language right now.

I'm not kidding. The people getting the most out of AI aren't coders. They're people who can articulate what they want.

Clear thinking becomes clear prompting. Clear prompting becomes useful output.

And nothing clarifies thinking faster than trying to say it out loud.

---

The skill this week is simple: a dictation-to-draft workflow.

It takes your raw voice memo transcript and transforms it into structured prose. Preserves your voice. Cleans up the "ums." Finds the through-line you were circling around.

You can use it with any transcription tool. The transcript goes in messy. The draft comes out clean.

---

Here's what changes when you adopt this:

**Volume goes up.** It's easier to record five minutes of talking than to write 500 words. You capture more ideas. More ideas means more raw material. More raw material means better output.

**Quality goes up.** Paradoxically, the messier input produces better results. Your authentic voice, captured in real time, has texture that typed prose lacks. AI preserves that texture while removing the noise.

**Friction goes down.** No more blank page syndrome. No more "what should I write about?" You already know what you think. You just need to hear yourself say it.

---

Try it this week.

Next time you have an idea, don't open a doc. Open your voice recorder. Talk for 5 minutes. Say whatever comes to mind. Don't worry about structure.

Then transcribe it and feed it to the skill.

You'll be surprised what's in there.
