Cal Newport's *Deep Work* had its moment, and for a certain kind of knowledge work - the kind where you sit alone with a problem for four hours and emerge with a solution - the thesis still holds. Protect your attention. Build a moat around your morning. The ability to focus without interruption is a superpower in a world that seems designed to interrupt you.

But something different happens when you work with AI agents, and I've been trying to figure out what to call it.

The old model assumed you were the one doing the work. You sat down, entered a flow state, produced output, and interruptions were the enemy because they pulled you out of that state - and everyone knows it takes twenty minutes to get back in. The research on this is clear, and Newport built a career on it.

Working with AI breaks this model in a way I didn't expect. You're not doing one thing for four hours anymore. You're spawning sub-agents, checking on their progress, course-correcting when they go sideways, spawning more. The rhythm is closer to jazz than to monastic contemplation - constant call-and-response, reading what the model gives you and riffing on it, redirecting when it misunderstands. If you try to do this with a Newport mindset, focusing on one agent until it completes its task, you'll waste hours watching a progress bar while your other threads sit idle.

I've started calling this *wide work*.

Not shallow work - that's something else entirely. Shallow work is scrolling Twitter, checking email compulsively, the thousand small distractions that fragment your attention into useless shards. Wide work is different. It's the ability to hold multiple threads in your mind while maintaining a sense of the whole, to zoom in on a detail and fix something and then zoom back out to see how that fix ripples through everything else.

Think of a conductor. When Leonard Bernstein led the New York Philharmonic, he wasn't "focusing" on the violin section in any meaningful sense. He held the entire orchestra in his mind at once - brass swelling here, strings diminishing there, timpani waiting for their moment three measures ahead. He moved his attention constantly, but not in the frazzled way we usually associate with multitasking. He could drop into any section, make an adjustment, and then pull back to hear the whole. The music required it.

The same move happens in a terminal when you're working with AI. You might have three agents running at once - one refactoring a piece of code, one researching a competitor, one drafting an email you've been putting off. You're moving between them, yes, but you're not context-switching in the way Newport warns against, that cognitive whiplash of jerking from one unrelated task to another. You're conducting. You're hearing all of it at once, even when you're focused on one part.

This is exactly what AI cannot do on its own. AI gets stuck. It loops. It grinds away at the wrong approach because it can't figure out which level of abstraction to operate at, doesn't know when to zoom out and reconsider whether the whole problem has been framed correctly. You can watch it happen in real time - the model will try the same failing strategy over and over, unable to step back.

But when you provide that nudge - "let's step back, I think we're approaching this wrong" - something unlocks. The model needed permission to zoom out, and you gave it. The human skill here is abstraction management: knowing which level to work at, when to push deeper, when to pull back and reconsider. That's the wide view, and it's something that doesn't come naturally to AI systems as they currently exist.

Rick Rubin has this quality, which is why I think of him as the prototypical vibe coder even though he's never written a line of code in his life. Rubin can barely operate a mixing board. He doesn't play instruments. But he knows when something is working and when it isn't, knows when to push the artist to try something different and when to get out of the way entirely. The technical details are someone else's problem. The perspective - the ability to zoom in and out at will, to hear the whole while attending to the part - that's his.

What distinguishes wide work from simply being frazzled? It's worth asking, because from the outside they might look similar. Think of the parent cooking dinner while answering work emails while helping a kid with homework. Same number of inputs as the conductor, same amount of attention-switching. Entirely different experience.

The frazzled parent experiences each interruption as an assault, another demand on already-depleted resources. The conductor experiences each instrument as information, another voice in a conversation that's going somewhere. When an agent pings you with a question, you can experience that as interruption - *another thing to deal with* - or you can experience it as collaboration, a thread reporting back. The difference is posture. Are you hunched over your desk, scrunched in, reactive to whatever hits you next? Or are you standing back, seeing the whole field, choosing where to zoom and when?

Deep work didn't disappear when I started working this way. It migrated.

The monk's cell used to be my desk with the door closed, four hours of protected focus on a single problem. Now it's the walk - an hour outside with my phone in my pocket, dictating notes into a transcription app when something crystallizes. That's where the big-picture thinking happens now, the synthesis and the vision and the sense of what I'm actually trying to accomplish. Back at the terminal, I execute on that vision. I conduct.

The composition happens on the trail. The performance happens at the keyboard.

So I've stopped trying to protect deep work time for AI tasks - that's fighting the last war. Instead I protect my thinking time, the walks and the showers and the mornings before I open my laptop, and I let the terminal sessions be what they want to be: wide, parallel, improvisational. I don't wait for one agent to finish before starting another. I don't hoard my attention like a scarce resource. I move it where it's needed, and I trust myself to hold the whole in mind.

Learn to zoom. That's the skill now.
