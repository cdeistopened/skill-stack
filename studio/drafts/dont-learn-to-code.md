Don't learn to code. Learn to flow.

Remember when they told coal miners in West Virginia to learn to code? The advice was condescending at the time - as if a fifty-year-old with a mortgage and a lifetime of physical labor could pivot to JavaScript bootcamps, as if the problem with Appalachia was simply a skills gap that could be fixed with the right online courses. It was obnoxious then. Now, looking back, it seems almost criminal. If those miners had actually followed that advice, had uprooted their lives and learned to code in 2018, they'd be staring down the barrel of another displacement by 2025. Retrained twice in a decade. The rug pulled out twice.

The skills that matter now are different, and they're not the kind you learn in a bootcamp.

A colleague told me recently that she was intimidated by Claude Code. She'd been watching people on Twitter throw around technical jargon - context windows, RAG pipelines, function calling, agentic workflows - and she'd assumed, reasonably enough, that she needed to understand all of it before she could even begin. That she'd trip over a term she didn't know and the whole thing would crash, or she'd reveal herself as a fraud who didn't belong.

She doesn't need to understand any of it. And that assumption - that you need to know the whole terrain before you can take a step - is exactly what holds people back.

Here's how it actually works: when you don't understand something, you ask. "What do you mean by context window?" you say, and the model tells you, and you move on. There's no test at the end. No gatekeeper checking your credentials. You learn by doing, you look things up when you get stuck, and you only step back to reconsider the whole approach when you're truly lost - when you've been going in circles and nothing is working.

The transcription revolution happened while nobody was paying attention, and it changed my relationship to writing more than any other technology of the past decade. For years, voice-to-text was a punchline. Dragon NaturallySpeaking mangling your sentences. Siri confidently mishearing everything. The technology existed, technically, but the friction made it useless for any serious work.

Then Whisper happened. OpenAI released it quietly in late 2022 - overshadowed by ChatGPT, which came out the same month - and suddenly transcription was fast, accurate, and essentially free. Apps like [Wispr Flow](/wispr-flow-vs-monologue) wrapped it in a clean interface: press a button, talk, watch your words appear on screen almost as fast as you can say them.

I've dictated over 900,000 words in Wispr Flow alone. Not because I'm lazy, but because talking is faster than typing and - more importantly - closer to thinking. When you type, you edit as you go. You backspace. You second-guess a word choice before you've finished the sentence. When you talk, you flow. The words come out rough, but they come out at the speed of thought, and the roughness doesn't matter because the AI is there to clean it up afterward.

This is the first half of the revolution: input at the speed of thought.

The second half is that English became a programming language. Large language models flipped the entire interface between humans and computers. Previously, if you wanted to make a computer do something complex, you had to speak its language - Python, JavaScript, SQL, whatever dialect the machine required. Now the machine speaks yours.

Andrej Karpathy, who used to run AI at Tesla, called English "the hottest new programming language." He was joking, but he was also right.

What most people miss about this shift is that you don't need to speak English perfectly. You don't need the ideal prompt, carefully crafted and polished before you hit enter. You need to speak continuously, iteratively, in conversation. Prompt engineering never became the discipline everyone predicted because it was never really about writing the perfect prompt - it was about the loop. A rough prompt, a response, an adjustment, another response, another adjustment, repeat. A thousand imperfect iterations will get you further than one perfect attempt ever could.

The best prompt engineers, it turned out, were just good conversationalists.

Perfectionism kills flow. That's the insight at the heart of what people call "vibe coding," even though the term makes it sound more frivolous than it is. The whole point is that you ship something rough and iterate on it. You say something half-formed, you see what comes back, you refine based on what you learn. The model has tools to ask you questions mid-task if it gets confused - it *wants* to clarify, it *wants* to understand what you're actually trying to do. Let it.

Ben Tossell, who runs the AI newsletter Ben's Bites, embodies this approach better than almost anyone I've seen. He's spent billions of tokens in Claude Code despite being, by his own admission, non-technical. "I still can't code," he writes, "but I understand a lot of the stack now." He learned backward - tools first, concepts as they became relevant, no prerequisites, no formal training. Just curiosity and the willingness to mess around and see what happened.

Zen Buddhists have a term for this: beginner's mind. The expert sees one way to solve a problem, the way they were trained, the way that worked before. The beginner sees many possibilities because they don't know yet which ones are supposed to be hard. When you don't know what's supposed to be impossible, you try things the expert would dismiss without a second thought.

The skills that actually matter now are the ones that can't be automated away, the ones that become more valuable as execution gets cheaper: the willingness to not know things and ask anyway, the creativity to see what could be built, the ability to cultivate a flow state and stay in it across a longer time horizon, the curiosity to poke at tools and ask "what happens if" instead of "what's the right way." These are human skills. They don't get displaced by the next model. They compound.

Don't wait until you understand RAG pipelines. Don't let the jargon intimidate you. Open a terminal, start talking, see what happens.

The conversation will teach you what you need to know.
