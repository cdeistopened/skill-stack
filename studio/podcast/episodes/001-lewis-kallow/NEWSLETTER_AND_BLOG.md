## Blog Post

### The Narrative Snippets Method: How to Extract Stories from Source Material

*Releasing alongside Episode 001 of the Skill Stack Podcast featuring Lewis Kallow*

---

Everyone I know is cheating on their work with Claude Code.

They're supposed to be doing their actual jobs - writing, managing, strategizing - and instead they're spending hours building things they never thought they could build. It feels like a guilty pleasure. It feels like cheating.

Lewis Kallow noticed the same thing. But before he got hooked on Claude Code, he had a prompting problem.

He was writing massive prompts - 2,000 words of detailed instructions. That's what everyone was telling him to do. But the outputs kept getting worse.

"I'd add more detail to fix it, and it would get even more generic," he told me on the podcast. The more he tried to explain what he wanted, the further the AI drifted from it.

Then he read about how George Lucas directed Star Wars. When Lucas landed in Tunisia with his crew, he didn't hand them a script and a mood board. He showed them four films: Flash Gordon serials, John Ford westerns, World War II dogfight footage, Kurosawa samurai movies.

"So I tried that with AI," Lewis said. "Just gave it five examples instead of explaining. Night and day difference."

This is the core insight behind what Lewis calls "narrative snippets" - a method for extracting stories from source material by recognizing universal patterns rather than describing them.

---

#### Who Is Lewis Kallow?

Lewis started writing about AI prompting during the pandemic, when he published ["How I Prompted My Way to Publish-Ready Content"](https://every.to/p/how-i-prompted-my-way-to-publish-ready-content) on Every.to - the publication that became a benchmark for thoughtful AI writing. That article laid out the examples-over-instructions principle that Dan Shipper later built into Spiral.

Before going public with his writing, Lewis was a ghostwriter for industry names. The thing about ghostwriting is you never get credit - which is by design. But the skills compound. He now co-writes The Action Digest with Scott Belsky, Adobe's Chief Strategy Officer and the founder of Behance. If you've used Belsky's Action Method notebooks, you've encountered the productivity philosophy that runs through their writing together.

---

#### The Problem with Describing What You Want

Most people prompt AI the way most directors would storyboard - with instructions. "Write something conversational but authoritative. Include a hook. Make it engaging."

The AI nods along and produces something generic. AI slop. The kind of output that needs so much editing it barely saves time.

Lewis and I have both independently arrived at the same conclusion: examples beat instructions. Lewis even found an academic study that analyzed over 500 prompting techniques - examples ranked number one. Show the AI what you want through demonstration, and it will pattern-match far more effectively than if you try to articulate every nuance.

But there's a layer beyond even that.

---

#### The Six Beats

When Lewis sent me his narrative snippets template, I realized he'd cracked something I'd been circling for months.

The best stories tend to follow a recognizable arc. Not every story, and not rigidly - but often enough that it's worth naming the pattern:

1. **Setup** - The protagonist in their world
2. **Disaster** - Something disrupts the status quo
3. **Failed Approach** - They try the obvious solution, it backfires
4. **New Insight** - A realization that changes everything
5. **Resolution** - They apply the insight, it works
6. **Reflection** - The universal takeaway

Look back at how I opened this article - with the help of the skill we developed together on our podcast collaboration.

Lewis writing 2,000-word prompts (setup).
Outputs getting worse (disaster).
Adding more detail making it more generic (failed approach).
The George Lucas method (insight).
Giving five examples instead of explaining (resolution).
Examples beat instructions (reflection).

The structure is invisible when it's working. But once you see it, you can't unsee it.

---

#### The Live Test

On the podcast, we tested this in real time.

I figured I'd try to get some actual work done during the conversation and apply Lewis's method to something I was stuck on.

I had a transcript from a recent episode with Joshua Fields Millburn of The Minimalists. I'd run it through my usual podcast workflow (skills stacked within skills, stacked within skills), but the cold open wasn't landing. Something about it felt warmed over.

So we applied Lewis's template - a Google doc containing two components: a fully deconstructed example story (the Howard Schultz origin story, with each beat labeled), and a quote extraction prompt for finding strong reflective moments in source material. I told Claude to find the most compelling story arc in the transcript and structure it using the six beats.

What came back was dramatically better than what I'd gotten from my standard prompt.

The AI found Joshua's story about discovering at age 40 that he was dyslexic - something he'd never known because he just assumed "reading was really difficult." It found the moment when his daughter Ella started struggling to read and he took the dyslexia test alongside her. "Oh," Joshua said in the transcript.

"Ooh."

That's a story. Setup, disaster, failed approach (assuming it was normal), insight (the test), resolution (understanding), reflection (what we label as weakness might be a misread environment).

The template didn't tell Claude what to write. It showed Claude what a story looks like when the bones are visible.

---

#### Why This Matters for Skills

Narrative snippets demonstrate something I've been thinking about for a while. They work because they combine three things that make any skill effective:

- **Examples** that show rather than tell
- **Structure** that guides without constraining
- **Context** loaded progressively, not all at once

Lewis's template, now converted into skill form, does all three. The six beats provide structure. The deconstructed examples show what each beat looks like in practice. And you only load the full template when you're actually extracting a story - not when you're doing something else.

A prompt is a single-use instruction. A skill is a reusable pattern that improves each time you use it.

> Prompts are disposable. Skills compound.

---

#### The Via Negativa

One more thing that came up in our conversation: the power of subtraction.

Lewis had referenced it in his original Every.to article - the idea that you often get better results by removing instructions than by adding them. I've started calling this the via negativa approach to skill design.

When you're building skills, the temptation is always to add. Add more instructions. Add more examples. Add more edge cases.

But skills degrade when they get bloated. The AI starts trying to satisfy too many constraints at once, and you get that same generic mush Lewis was fighting against with his 2,000-word prompts.

I learned this the hard way with my own cold open skill. The original version had twelve rules for what makes a good hook. The AI would dutifully check all twelve boxes and produce something technically correct but lifeless. I cut it to five. The outputs got better. I cut it to three. Better still.

Better to keep the template tight. Six beats. A few deconstructed examples. Trust the pattern.

---

#### Try It

I'm releasing the Narrative Snippets skill alongside this episode.

The workflow is simple:

1. Feed it a transcript, interview, or raw notes
2. Ask it to identify story arcs that fit the six beats
3. Choose the strongest one
4. Reshape it for your target format (cold open, blog intro, social post)

If you're working with podcast transcripts, interview content, or any source material that contains hidden stories, this will produce better results than asking Claude to "write something engaging."

The content treadmill grinds people down because they're always generating from scratch. Skills let you step off. You build the pattern once, then run your raw material through it.

George Lucas knew something about directing that applies to prompting. Don't describe what you want. Show it.
